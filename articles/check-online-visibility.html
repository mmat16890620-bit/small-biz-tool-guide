How Local Businesses Can Check Their Online Visibility

When a potential customer looks for a business like yours, what do they actually see? Not your website's homepage as you picture it — they see a search results page: a map with a few pinned businesses, a panel with your name, hours, and star rating, and a list of links where competitors may sit above you. "Online visibility" is simply how present and how accurate your business is on that page. The good news is that checking it requires no expertise and, for the core routine, no money. This guide walks through the checks in order, from the five-minute version to the monthly habit, and then explains where a paid tool genuinely saves time.

The short version: Search for your business the way a stranger would — by name, and by service plus town. Claim and complete your free Google Business Profile. Write down where you rank for your five most important searches. Check your reviews and your name/address/phone consistency across directories. Do that monthly and you have covered most of what visibility tools automate.

Step 1: Search for your business like a customer

Start with three searches, on your phone, because most local searches happen on phones:

  1. Your business name. Does the panel that appears show the right name, address, phone number, hours, and photos? Is it your listing at all — or a similarly named business across town?
  2. Your main service plus your town — for example, "hair salon Austin" or "emergency plumber Leeds." Where do you appear: in the map results, further down the page, or not at all? Note which competitors appear above you.
  3. "Near me" searches — "coffee near me," "dentist near me." These depend on the searcher's location, so try them from your shop and, if you can, from across town.

Two technique points matter. First, search results are personalized, so your own business often appears higher for you than for a stranger; using a private/incognito browser window gives a cleaner, if not perfect, picture. Second, results vary by location, so a single check from your desk is a sample, not the whole truth. That limitation is exactly what the later steps — and eventually tools — help with.

Step 2: Claim and complete your Google Business Profile

Your Google Business Profile is the free listing that powers the map results and the info panel in Step 1. If you have never claimed it, do that first at google.com/business — Google will verify that you represent the business, typically by video or postcard depending on the business type. Then work through the basics:

  • Name, address, phone — exactly as they appear on your signage and website. Inconsistencies confuse both Google and customers.
  • Primary category — pick the category that describes what the business is ("Bakery"), not everything it sells, and add secondary categories where they genuinely apply.
  • Hours — including holiday and special hours. An incorrect "closed" label costs real visits.
  • Photos — the storefront (so people can find you), the interior, and your work or products. Listings with real photos look open; listings without look dormant.
  • Services, attributes, and description — fill in what the profile offers: services list, accessibility or payment attributes, and a plain description of what you do and where.

Google's own Business Profile help center documents each field. Treat this profile as a living page: update hours when they change, add photos seasonally, and keep it matching your website.

Step 3: Check your positions for the searches that matter

Visibility is not one number; it is a handful of specific searches. Pick the five to ten phrases a customer would realistically type — your main services plus your town or neighborhood — and check each one the way you did in Step 1. Record the results in a simple spreadsheet: the date, the phrase, whether you appeared in the map results, your position in the regular results, and who was above you. Repeat on the same day each month. A single check tells you where you stand today; the monthly series tells you whether you are gaining or slipping, and whether a change you made — new photos, a corrected category, more reviews — had any effect. If you have a website, Google Search Console (also free) complements this by showing which queries actually surfaced your site and how often people clicked.

Step 4: Check your reviews — count, rating, and recency

For many local searches, the star rating next to your name is the first filter a customer applies. Check three things: your average rating, your total number of reviews compared with the competitors who appear beside you, and how recent the latest reviews are. A 4.8 average from reviews written three years ago reads as a closed business; a steady trickle of recent reviews reads as an active one. Then check that you are responding — a short, polite reply to reviews, including critical ones, is visible to every future customer who reads them. Finally, look beyond Google: depending on your trade, customers may find you on Yelp, TripAdvisor, Facebook, or an industry directory, and a neglected profile on any of them tells its own story.

Step 5: Check your details are consistent everywhere

Your business name, address, and phone number appear in dozens of places you did not create: map services, directories, review sites, social pages. Search for your phone number and your address and see what comes up. Old addresses from before a move, a mistyped phone number, or a duplicate listing under a slightly different name all dilute your visibility and send some customers to the wrong place. Correct what you can claim, and report duplicates for removal. This is tedious by hand — remember it when we talk about tools below — but even a one-time pass over the top directories removes the worst errors.

A monthly routine you can keep

  • Week 1 (15 minutes): Run your Step 1 searches. Note your map and results positions for your five key phrases in the spreadsheet.
  • Week 2 (10 minutes): Open your Google Business Profile. Confirm hours, add a recent photo, check that nothing has been edited by others or marked incorrectly.
  • Week 3 (10 minutes): Read and reply to new reviews on Google and your main secondary platform. If reviews have stalled, ask recent happy customers — in person works best.
  • Week 4 (10 minutes): Spot-check your name, address, and phone on the top directories and map services. Fix one inconsistency if you find one.

Under an hour a month, and each pass makes the next one faster, because you are comparing against your own notes instead of starting from a blank page.

Bottom line

Online visibility is not a mystery metric; it is what a stranger sees when they search, and you can look at it yourself, free, in an afternoon. Claim your Google Business Profile, keep it accurate, watch a short list of searches monthly, stay current with reviews, and keep your details consistent. When the manual checks start eating hours — more locations, more keywords, more directories — that is the honest moment to let a tool like Semrush automate the tracking. Until then, the routine above is the real thing, not a teaser for it.
